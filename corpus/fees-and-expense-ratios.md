# Fees and Expense Ratios

Fees may look small, but over time they take a large bite out of returns because the money paid in
fees is no longer invested and no longer compounding. A fund's expense ratio is its annual operating
cost expressed as a percentage of assets; a 1.00 percent expense ratio means you pay 10 dollars a
year for every 1,000 dollars invested, deducted automatically from the fund.

The difference between a low-cost and a high-cost fund compounds dramatically over decades. The U.S.
SEC has illustrated that even a fraction of a percent in annual fees can reduce a portfolio's final
value by tens of thousands of dollars over an investing lifetime. Because index funds are passively
managed, they typically carry far lower expense ratios than actively managed funds.

Watch for other costs too: sales loads (commissions paid when you buy or sell some funds), account
or advisory fees, and trading costs. Lower costs are one of the few advantages an investor can
control and lock in, unlike future returns, which no one can predict. When comparing similar
investments, the lower-cost option starts with a built-in head start.
