# Treasury Securities

Treasury securities are debt issued by the U.S. federal government. Treasury bills are short-term,
Treasury notes are medium-term, and Treasury bonds are long-term. Because they are backed by the
full faith and credit of the United States, they are widely regarded as carrying very low credit
risk, which is one reason their yields are often used as a benchmark for other investments.

Some Treasury products are designed to protect against inflation. Treasury Inflation-Protected
Securities, known as TIPS, adjust their principal value with changes in a measure of consumer
prices, so the payments rise and fall with inflation. Series I savings bonds also earn a return
that is tied in part to inflation. These products aim to help preserve purchasing power over time.

Individual investors can buy many of these securities directly from the government through
TreasuryDirect, often without a broker. As with any fixed-income investment, the market value of a
Treasury security can change before maturity as interest rates move, even though the government's
promise to repay the principal at maturity does not change.
