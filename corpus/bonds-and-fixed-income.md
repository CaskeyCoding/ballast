# Bonds and Fixed Income

A bond is a loan from an investor to a borrower, such as a government or a company. In return for
the money, the borrower agrees to pay interest over a set period and to repay the original amount,
called the principal, when the bond matures. Because the payments are defined in advance, bonds are
often described as fixed-income investments.

Bonds carry their own risks. Interest-rate risk means that when prevailing interest rates rise, the
market price of an existing bond usually falls, since newer bonds pay more. Credit risk is the
chance that the borrower fails to make payments, which is why bonds from financially weaker issuers
generally pay higher interest to compensate. Longer-term bonds tend to be more sensitive to
interest-rate changes than shorter-term ones.

In a portfolio, bonds are often used to add income and to balance the larger swings of stocks,
because bond and stock prices do not always move together. Common types include government bonds,
municipal bonds issued by states and localities, and corporate bonds. The right mix depends on an
investor's goals, time horizon, and tolerance for risk.
